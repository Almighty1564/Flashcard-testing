Where the energy actually goes

A low f/D is a deep bowl that wraps around the feed, so the rim sits at a wide angle and the horn must spray broadly to light it. A high f/D is a shallow dish with the feed far out front, needing a narrow horn pattern. The depth follows from the other two: c = D² / 16F.

Why the beam comes out narrow

The feed radiates a spherical wave in all directions. Every path from the focus, off the reflector, out to the aperture plane is exactly the same length — that is the defining property of a parabola. Same length means same travel time, so the whole wave arrives at the mouth of the dish in step, flattened into a plane wave marching in one direction. A wide, flat, in-step wavefront is precisely what a narrow beam is.

Boresight
The axis the dish is aimed along, straight out of the centre of the reflector. It is where gain peaks, and it is the zero that every other angle is measured from. Pointing a terminal is putting the satellite on boresight.
Main beam
The single large lobe around boresight, ending at the first null. Nearly all the useful power lives here.
Sidelobe
Any smaller lobe outside the main beam. Power you did not aim anywhere useful, and the path by which you interfere with, or get interfered with by, an adjacent satellite.
Null
An angle where the lobes meet and gain collapses. Point a null at an interferer and you stop hearing it.
Half-power beamwidth
How wide the main beam is, measured where gain has fallen by 3 dB — half the power. It is the practical answer to how tightly you must hold your pointing.
dBi
Gain compared against an isotropic radiator, the dashed circle in the polar view.

The dashed circle in the polar view is an isotropic radiator — the same power spread evenly in every direction, and the reference every gain figure is measured against. That is what the i in dBi means. Everything the pattern pushes out along boresight it had to take from somewhere else, and the sidelobes are where the leftovers land. They are why a dish aimed at one satellite can still hear a second one a few degrees off.
