How brightly the feed lights the rim compared with the centre. Section 4 is entirely about this number.

1 · The shape of the reflector

A dish is a paraboloid, and it is fully described by two numbers: the diameter D across the mouth, and the focal length F from the bottom of the bowl out to the feed. The depth c is not a free choice — it follows from those two as c = D²/16F. Drag f/D and watch a shallow dish turn into a deep bowl that wraps around its own feed.

2 · Why the beam comes out narrow

The feed radiates a spherical wave — the curved arcs. Every path from the feed, off the reflector, out to the aperture plane is the same length, so every part of the wave arrives at the mouth of the dish at the same moment. Watch an arc flatten as it reflects: that is a spherical wave being turned into a plane wave. A wide, flat, in-step wavefront is what a narrow beam is made of.

3 · Lighting the dish: the trade nobody escapes

Point a horn at a reflector and you cannot win twice. Spray wide enough to light the rim brightly and a lot of the beam misses the dish entirely — that wasted power is spillover. Narrow the horn to stop the waste and the outer part of the dish goes dark, so you paid for metal you are not using. The left panel is the dish seen face-on, lit as the feed actually lights it. 4 · Reading a radiation pattern

Distance from the centre means gain, not distance in space. The further the trace sits from the middle, the more power goes that way. The dashed circle is an isotropic radiator — the same power spread evenly in every direction — and it is the reference every dBi figure is measured against. Drag anywhere on the plot to read the gain at that angle.

This one is deliberately drawn for a small dish, about nine wavelengths across, so the lobes stay big enough to see and name. A real terminal dish is far larger, which squeezes the main beam into the needle you see in the 3D view at the top — same shape, same rules, just far narrower.

The words

Boresight
The axis the dish is aimed along, straight out of the middle of the reflector. Gain peaks here, and every other angle is measured from it. Pointing a terminal is putting the satellite on boresight.
Main beam
The one large lobe around boresight, ending at the first null. Nearly all the useful power is in here.
Sidelobe
Any smaller lobe outside the main beam. Power aimed nowhere useful, and the route by which you interfere with the satellite next door.
Spillover
Feed energy that misses the reflector completely and carries on into space. It leaves as a cone off to the side, never straight out the back — straight back is solid metal.
Null
An angle where lobes meet and gain collapses. Put a null on an interferer and you stop hearing it.
Half-power beamwidth
How wide the main beam is, measured where gain has fallen 3 dB. It is the practical answer to how accurately you must point.
Aperture efficiency
How much of the dish's physical area you actually get to use. 50–70 % is normal on a real antenna.
dBi
Gain measured against an isotropic radiator.
